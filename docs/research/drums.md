# Drum Synthesis — research & lessons learned

What we tried, what we learned, and what to do differently when we come back to drums.

> **Status:** drum machine sketch removed after exploration. The synthesis approach needs
> more work. Come back to drums after the core audio engine (proper envelopes, waveforms,
> filter) is in place — that same foundation makes drums easier to get right.

---

## What we tried (sketch 11-drum-machine)

A unified `DrumVoice` struct mixing a sine oscillator and white noise, with a 1-pole IIR
high-pass filter. Four voices: kick, snare, closed hi-hat, clap.

**What worked:**
- The architecture concept (oscillator + filtered noise per voice) is correct for 808-style synthesis.
- xorshift32 noise generator: good quality, cheap. Better than `rand()` at audio rates.
- The 1-pole HP filter formula is valid and cheap.
- Separating parameters into a struct and trigger helpers made the voices easy to swap.

**What didn't work:**

| Problem | Root cause | Fix needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All decays sound unnatural/buzzy | Linear decay — `env -= rate` | Exponential: `env *= coeff` where `coeff = expf(-1/(SR × t))` |
| Kick has no punch or thud | Linear pitch sweep, too slow, too gentle | Exponential pitch drop, must happen in < 10 ms not 55 ms |
| Snare sounds wrong | One sine body + HP noise isn't enough | Two detuned oscillators + bandpass (not HP) on the noise |
| Hi-hat sounds like TV static | White noise + 1-pole HP = flat hiss | Needs multiple detuned oscillators (see TR-909 section below) |
| Everything too loud | Fixed-gain PAM8403 amp; DRUM_VOL scale off | Lower scale, or use headphone out for testing |

## How reference devices actually do it

### Teenage Engineering PO-33 K.O! — sampler, not a synthesizer

No synthesis at all. It records short audio clips from a microphone or line-in and plays
them back at different pitches via a time-stretching algorithm. The drums sound good
because they are real recordings of real drums. Comparing PO-33 to synthesis is
comparing a tape recorder to an oscillator.

**Takeaway for this project:** if we want PO-33-quality drum sounds, we need sample
playback. The ESP32-S3 has 8 MB of flash; a 0.5 s drum sample at 44.1 kHz / 16-bit
takes ~88 KB, so 40–60 samples fit comfortably. That's a different feature, not synthesis.

### Stylophone Beat — sample playback

Pre-loaded 8/12-bit PCM samples stored in ROM, played back at fixed pitch with basic
envelope shaping. Same conclusion as PO-33: the good sound comes from real recordings,
not from equations.

### Roland TR-808 — fully analog synthesis

The 808 is the most important synthesized drum machine reference. All voices are
generated by analog circuits, and the sound quality comes from the *physics of those
circuits*, not from clever DSP:

- **Kick:** a bridged-T oscillator (a specific analog topology) that produces a sine-like
  wave. The pitch drops because a capacitor discharges — an inherently exponential curve.
  The "boom" is the long capacitor tail. The "click" at the attack is the oscillator
  turning on and the capacitor charging spike.
- **Snare:** two bridged-T oscillators at different frequencies + white noise through a
  bandpass filter. The *two-oscillator* body is what gives the snare its "snap."
- **Hi-hat:** white noise only, but filtered through a resonant bandpass that gives it a
  metallic shimmer beyond what a simple HP can produce.

**Key insight:** the 808 sounds the way it does because of exponential capacitor physics
and real analog nonlinearities (self-oscillation, soft clipping). Digital software
replication needs exponential envelopes and waveshaping at minimum.

### Roland TR-909 — hybrid (analog kick/snare + digital hi-hat/cymbal)

- Kick and snare: analog circuits (simpler than 808, different character).
- Hi-hat, cymbal, rim shot: 12-bit digital samples from ROM.
- The 909 hi-hat in particular is famous because it is **six square-wave oscillators at
  inharmonic frequency ratios**, mixed together — not white noise. The inharmonic mixture
  creates metallic shimmer. Pure white noise never sounds quite like it.

### Roland TR-606 "Drumatix" — fully analog, simpler than 808

Released 1981 as the companion to the TB-303. Cheaper and simpler than the 808:

- Kick: a relaxation oscillator (capacitor discharge) — shorter, clickier than 808.
  Less low-end boom, more attack. Sits differently in the mix.
- Snare: simpler one-oscillator design + noise.
- Character: trashy, punchy, "lo-fi" compared to the 808 — which is why it became a
  staple in early house and techno. The 606 kick leaves room for a bassline.

**Why this matters for us:** the 606 kick is an easier synthesis target than the 808. It
is shorter, less exponential-tail-dependent, and more "electronic/lo-fi" — which fits the
groovebox brief. If we synthesize drums, the 606 is the better starting point than the 808.

full tracks, to understand what each machine actually sounds like.

| Roland TR-808 | 1980 | Boomy kick, snappy snare, metallic hats | The template. Everything is compared to this. |

## What to build when we come back to drums

The right foundation is the **core audio engine** (see synthesis.md build path and the
architecture plan). Once we have:

1. Exponential ADSR envelopes (the single most important fix)
2. Multiple waveform types (sine, saw, square, triangle)
3. A state-variable filter (SVF) — gives LP, HP, BP simultaneously

...synthesizing a TR-606-style kick becomes:

```
Kick  = SVF(sine_oscillator, BP) + exponential_pitch_env + exponential_amp_env
Snare = 2× detuned sine + SVF(noise, BP) + exponential_env per component
Hat   = 6× detuned square at inharmonic ratios + SVF(HP) + short exp_env
```

These are direct applications of the same voice building blocks, not a separate system.

**Alternative path:** sample playback from SPI flash or internal flash. Easier to
implement, sounds better immediately. A hybrid approach (synthesis for pitched sounds,
samples for drums) is how the TR-909 solved exactly this problem.
